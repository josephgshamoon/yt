**Channel:** Hidden Realms · **Format:** 10:00 — 60 blocks × 10s, mannequin style, 16:9
**Story:** The Museum of the Bible scandal — the forged Dead Sea Scroll fragments and the Oxford papyri thefts. Documented: museum's own 2020 forensic report; the Obbink/EES case; DOJ repatriations.
**Narrator:** same dry documentary voice (Cillian preset)

**Alt titles:**
- The Billionaire Who Bought a Fake Bible
- Every Single One Was Fake

## ACT 1 — COLD OPEN (1–6)

1. In 2017, a billion-dollar museum opened in Washington D.C., three blocks from the Capitol. Its crown jewels: sixteen scraps of the oldest Bible on Earth. **[Museum exterior, grand atrium]**
2. The Dead Sea Scrolls. Two thousand years old. The family that built the museum had paid millions for them — through dealers who asked for discretion. **[Fragment in glass case, spotlight]**
3. Three years later, a forensic team published their verdict on all sixteen fragments. Not one was ancient. Not one was real. Every single fragment was a modern fake. **[Report page stamped across the case]**
4. Ink on old leather. Painted, possibly, in our lifetime — by someone who understood exactly what wealthy believers wanted to buy. **[Close: ink bleeding into cracked leather]**
5. And while the museum reeled, a second scandal was breaking across the ocean — an Oxford professor accused of selling stolen Gospel papyri out of a university archive. **[Oxford spires, archive boxes]**
6. This is the file on the fake Bible trade: the billionaire, the forger nobody has named, and the professor who sold what wasn't his. **[Evidence wall: three silhouette cards; title card]**

## ACT 2 — THE COLLECTOR (7–16)

7. Start with the buyer. The Green family of Oklahoma City — owners of Hobby Lobby, a craft-store empire worth billions, and among America's most devout Christian families. **[Corporate HQ, family silhouettes in prayer]**
8. Their dream had a shape: a national museum that would put the Bible beside the Constitution in the American capital — backed by the largest private biblical collection on Earth. **[Blueprint unrolling into a museum model]**
9. So they went shopping. In under a decade, the family amassed some forty thousand artifacts — tablets, torahs, papyri — spending at a pace the antiquities world had never seen. **[Conveyor of crates, auction paddles]**
10. Dealers noticed. When a buyer wants biblical treasures, believes deeply, and moves fast — the market will manufacture whatever the buyer is praying to find. **[Grey dealer mannequins whispering, handshakes]**
11. The warnings came early. In 2011, customs seized shipments of clay tablets addressed to Hobby Lobby — labeled, on the paperwork, as ceramic tile samples. **[Customs table, opened crate, tablets in straw]**
12. The tablets had been smuggled out of Iraq. The company paid a three-million-dollar settlement and returned thousands of artifacts. A warning shot — but the collecting continued. **[Documents stamped; crates loaded back]**
13. Because the collection's centerpiece was already secured: sixteen fragments of the Dead Sea Scrolls, bought between 2009 and 2014 in quiet private sales. **[Sixteen small cases in a velvet row]**
14. The real Scrolls are the most famous manuscripts on Earth — found by shepherds in desert caves in 1947, two thousand years old, the oldest Bible ever touched. **[Desert cliffs, cave mouth, clay jars]**
15. Almost all of them sit locked in Jerusalem, national treasures that no fortune can buy. Which raises the question the buyers didn't ask loudly enough. **[Vault door closing on scroll cases]**
16. Where did sixteen new fragments suddenly come from? The dealers' answer was a name every collector knew: the Kando family — with a vault in Switzerland. **[Ledger line: "private Swiss vault", sepia legend tint]**

## ACT 3 — THE PERFECT PROVENANCE (17–28)

17. Khalil Iskander Shahin — "Kando" — was the Bethlehem antiquities dealer who, in 1947, brokered the original Scrolls from the shepherds who found them. **[1940s shop, oil lamps, scroll jar on counter]**
18. The story went: Kando kept a few fragments back, the family smuggled them to Switzerland, and now — three generations later — they were quietly for sale. **[Suitcase crossing a border checkpoint, map line to Geneva]**
19. It was the perfect provenance. Impossible to verify, impossible to disprove, and wrapped in the one name buyers wanted to hear. Collectors lined up. **[Queue of buyer mannequins outside a vault door]**
20. And not only the Greens. After 2002, around seventy "new" Dead Sea Scroll fragments surfaced on the market. Seminaries bought. Universities bought. Millionaires bought. **[Fragments multiplying across a map of buyers]**
21. Prices ran from hundreds of thousands to millions per scrap — for pieces smaller than a credit card. Faith, scarcity, and secrecy: the perfect market. **[Tiny scrap on scale outweighing gold coins]**
22. The Greens bought their sixteen and built them a shrine: climate-controlled cases in a five-hundred-million-dollar museum rising in Washington. **[Construction cranes, glass cases installed]**
23. November 2017. The Museum of the Bible opens. Four hundred and thirty thousand square feet. The scroll gallery is its holy of holies. **[Ribbon cut, crowds, the dim scroll room]**
24. But scholars were already whispering. The fragments' script looked odd — letters that wobbled where ancient scribes were steady, strokes that followed the damage instead of predating it. **[Magnifier over wobbling letters]**
25. Think about that last detail. Ink that flows into cracks which took two thousand years to form — means the cracks were there before the ink. **[Animation: crack forms, THEN ink flows in]**
26. One scholar called the collection's script "drawn, not written." Another counted five different fragments quoting the exact passages evangelical buyers most cherished. Convenient scripture. **[Five fragments glowing on favorite verses]**
27. The museum did something institutions rarely do with their crown jewels: it sent five fragments to a German lab, then hired an independent forensic team for all sixteen. **[Fragments packed under seal, lab door]**
28. The team spent months with 3D microscopes, X-ray fluorescence, infrared. In March 2020, at a conference in Washington, they delivered the verdict. **[Microscope eye descending on parchment]**

## ACT 4 — THE VERDICT (29–38)

29. All sixteen fragments are modern forgeries. Not "doubtful." Not "some." All. The museum's own hired experts, announcing its crown jewels were paint. **[Report cover: "conclusively... forgeries"]**
30. The tell was the material itself. Real Scrolls are parchment. These were ancient *leather* — likely cut from genuinely old scraps, even Roman-era shoes and sandals. **[Ancient shoe leather, scissors tracing fragment shapes]**
31. Genuinely ancient canvas, freshly painted. The forger had used two-thousand-year-old leather so carbon dating would swear the material was authentic. **[Lab clock: "material: ancient ✓ ink: modern ✗"]**
32. Under the microscope, a golden sheen: animal-skin glue, brushed on in modern times to mimic the surface of true parchment — and to hold ink the leather would otherwise reject. **[Golden shimmer under magnification]**
33. And the ink pooled in the cracks. On every fragment, the writing obeyed the damage. Whoever wrote these was writing on ruins — recently. **[Ink settling into fissures, repeated across fragments]**
34. Sixteen for sixteen. The Greens had reportedly paid millions. The question left standing wasn't whether they'd been fooled. It was: by whom? **[Sixteen cases going dark one by one]**
35. Here the file goes quiet. The forger has never been publicly identified. The fragments passed through chains of dealers, each pointing backward at the last. **[Chain of hand-offs receding into fog]**
36. What the trail shows is skill: knowledge of scribal habits, access to ancient leather, patience — and a sharp read of what the market wanted. Scholars suspect a forger trained in the field itself. **[Workbench: brushes, leather scraps, reference books]**
37. The other seventy fragments sold since 2002? Most experts now presume the entire post-2002 crop is fake. Seminaries and universities quietly pulled theirs from display. **[Display cases across a map going dark]**
38. Insurance, lawsuits, silence. Institutions rarely advertise the price of being fooled. The market simply — moved on. **[Filing cabinet drawer closing on claims]**

## ACT 5 — THE PROFESSOR (39–50)

39. Which brings us to the second file — because while the scroll verdict broke, the Greens were entangled in another scandal. This one had a name attached. **[Second folder slides across the desk]**
40. Dirk Obbink. Oxford professor, MacArthur "genius grant" winner, one of the greatest papyrologists alive — a man who could read garbage-heap scraps like morning newspapers. **[Book-lined Oxford office, purple-robed mannequin]**
41. Oxford's Oxyrhynchus collection — half a million papyrus fragments dug from Egyptian sands — sat partly in his care. Unpublished. Uncatalogued in full. Tempting. **[Rows of archive boxes to the ceiling]**
42. Between 2010 and 2013, Obbink allegedly sold dozens of those fragments — including pieces of the Gospels — to one buyer above all: Hobby Lobby. **[Gloved hands lifting papyri into a briefcase]**
43. Among them, reportedly, a fragment of Mark's Gospel that rumor had crowned "first-century Mark" — the oldest gospel scrap on Earth — whispered about at scholarly dinners for years. **[Single papyrus under a rumor spotlight]**
44. The whisper was the sales pitch. When the fragment was finally published, it was dated a century later — and it turned out to belong to Oxford all along. **[Date stamp shifting; ownership label: EES]**
45. In 2019, the Egypt Exploration Society compared its records against Hobby Lobby's purchases: at least thirteen of its papyri had allegedly been sold out of the archive. Later counts said more. **[Two ledgers side by side, lines lighting up red]**
46. Obbink denied everything. Oxford suspended, then dismissed him. He was arrested, released, and — years on — never charged with a crime. **[Empty office chair, name scraped from door]**
47. Hobby Lobby sued him for seven million dollars. The papyri went back to Oxford. The scholar at the center went silent — reportedly living on a houseboat. **[Court filing; houseboat on grey water]**
48. Two scandals, one lesson. It didn't matter that the buyers were sincere. Sincerity is what the market priced in. **[Split screen: fake scrolls / stolen papyri]**
49. The Museum of the Bible, to its credit, kept the receipts public: it published the forensic report, returned thousands of disputed artifacts to Iraq and Egypt, and put empty cases on display. **[Crates airborne over the ocean; an empty lit case]**
50. An empty museum case, labeled honestly, may be the rarest artifact in this entire story. **[The empty case, long hold]**

## ACT 6 — THE MORAL + CLOSE (51–60)

51. Because forgery isn't new. Every century gets the fakes it deserves — relics for medieval pilgrims, shroud after shroud, enough splinters of the True Cross to build a ship. **[Montage: relic cases through the ages]**
52. The nineteenth century had Constantine Simonides, who forged entire "ancient" gospels — and whose fakes still surface in collections today. **[Quill forging Greek letters by candlelight]**
53. The pattern never changes. The forger doesn't sell parchment. The forger sells confirmation — proof that what you already believe is true. **[Mirror held up to a buyer mannequin]**
54. That's why the fragments quoted the favorite verses. Why the provenance ran through a beloved name. Why the buyers moved fast and asked gently. **[The three tells pinned to the evidence wall]**
55. And it's why the scientists matter. Ink in a crack doesn't care what anyone believes. The microscope read the fragments the way faith could not. **[Microscope light over the fragment, cold and steady]**
56. The Dead Sea Scrolls themselves remain exactly what they were: real, ancient, and safely catalogued — untouched by the fraud that borrowed their name. **[The true scrolls in their Jerusalem vault]**
57. So the file closes on three facts. Sixteen fragments, all fake, forger unknown. Dozens of papyri, allegedly sold by their own keeper. And a museum that chose to say so out loud. **[Three cards pinned in a row]**
58. Somewhere, a workshop produced the most successful biblical forgeries of our era — and its owner has watched this entire story unfold, unnamed. **[Empty workbench, brush still wet]**
59. If the post-2002 fragments fooled the experts for nearly two decades… the question isn't whether there are more fakes in the world's collections. It's how many. **[Endless archive shelves fading to dark]**
60. Faith built the museum. Science emptied the case. Between the two sits everything this channel is about. Which fragment would have fooled you? Subscribe — the next file is already open. **[The empty case; slow pull back to black; end card]**
