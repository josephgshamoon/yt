# SCRIPT — "The Man Who Saved the Internet (Then the FBI Arrested Him)"

**Style:** Blackfiles-type long-form hacker documentary
**Target runtime:** ~22 minutes (~3,200 words VO @ ~150 wpm + b-roll pauses)
**Based on the true story of:** Marcus Hutchins ("MalwareTech") and the WannaCry attack, May 2017
**Alternate titles (A/B thumbnails):**
- *He Stopped the World's Biggest Cyberattack for $10*
- *The 22-Year-Old Who Saved the Internet — Then Got Arrested*

**Thumbnail concept:** Split image — left: hooded figure in front of a wall of red "RANSOMWARE" screens; right: same figure in handcuffs, FBI jacket blurred in background. Text: "HE SAVED THE INTERNET. THEY ARRESTED HIM."

## COLD OPEN [0:00 – 1:10]

**[VISUAL: Black screen. A single red timer ticking down. SFX: hospital heart monitor, distant sirens.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
May 12th, 2017. 8:24 a.m.

Inside hospitals across the United Kingdom, computer screens begin turning red. One by one. Then hundreds at a time.

Surgeries are cancelled mid-preparation. Ambulances are turned away from emergency rooms. Doctors are locked out of patient records — and on every frozen screen, the same message:

**[VISUAL: The actual WannaCry ransom screen. Zoom slowly into the text.]**

*"Oops, your files have been encrypted. You have 3 days to pay."*

By lunchtime, it isn't just hospitals. It's banks in Russia. Railways in Germany. Factories in France so infected they stop the assembly lines. A hundred and fifty countries. More than two hundred thousand machines. The fastest-spreading cyberattack the world has ever seen — and nobody can stop it.

**[VISUAL: World map lighting up red, country by country. Music building.]**

Nobody… except a twenty-two-year-old college dropout, sitting in his bedroom at his parents' house in a small English surf town — who is about to kill the biggest cyberattack in history…

…by spending ten dollars and sixty-nine cents.

**[MUSIC CUT. Beat of silence.]**

Three months later, the FBI arrested him.

**[VISUAL: Title card slams in: THE MAN WHO SAVED THE INTERNET.]**

This is how the same man became both the hero of the internet — and a wanted cybercriminal. And why both of those things… were true.

## ACT 1 — THE KID IN THE ATTIC [1:10 – 5:30]

**[VISUAL: Drone shots of Ilfracombe, Devon — cliffs, grey sea, small town. Slow, calm music.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
To understand how one person ends up holding the internet's kill switch, we have to go back — to a town most people in England couldn't find on a map.

Ilfracombe. Population: eleven thousand. The kind of place where the biggest event of the year is the surfing weather.

This is where Marcus Hutchins grew up. And from the outside, he was a normal kid — tall, quiet, obsessed with surfing. But at night, Marcus lived somewhere else entirely.

**[VISUAL: Old 2000s-era computer, CRT glow in a dark bedroom. Re-enactment b-roll.]**

At twelve, he wrote his first code. At thirteen, when his school's IT admins blocked the students' favorite websites, Marcus didn't complain. He found a way around the firewall — and then, quietly, shared it with the entire school.

That's the thing you need to understand about Marcus. He wasn't drawn to computers. He was drawn to *locked doors*.

**[VISUAL: Forum screenshots, blurred usernames. HackForums-style UI.]**

And on the internet of the late 2000s, there was an entire world built for kids like him. Hacking forums. Places where teenagers traded code the way other kids traded football cards. Marcus — hiding behind a username — started building things to impress the older members.

First, harmless tools. Then… less harmless ones.

By fourteen, he had written a program that could steal saved passwords from a browser. He says he didn't think about the people on the other end. It was a puzzle. Points on a scoreboard. The forum cheered — so he kept going.

**[MUSIC: darker undertone begins.]**

And here's where the story splits in two. Because at the exact same time Marcus was sliding into the underground… he was also becoming something else.

Under the name **MalwareTech**, he started a blog — where he took viruses apart and published exactly how they worked. Security professionals read it. Companies read it. He was nineteen, with no degree, no certifications, living in his parents' house…

…and one of the best malware analysts in the world was reading his blog and taking notes.

**[BEAT.]**

One version of Marcus was helping build weapons. The other was teaching the world how to disarm them.

Sooner or later, one of those two lives was going to catch up with him.

**[TRANSITION STING: "But first — half the world had to catch fire."]**

## ACT 2 — THE WEAPON [5:30 – 10:00]

**[VISUAL: NSA headquarters at Fort Meade, ominous low angle. Documents with REDACTED bars.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The attack that made Marcus famous didn't start in a basement. It started inside the most powerful intelligence agency on Earth.

For years, the NSA had been quietly collecting *vulnerabilities* — secret flaws in the software the entire world runs on. Think of them as master keys. And the crown jewel was a flaw in Microsoft Windows so powerful the agency named it **EternalBlue**.

Here's the analogy: imagine every office building in the world used the same brand of door lock. EternalBlue was a key that opened all of them. Silently. From anywhere. No alarm, no broken glass — the door just opens.

**[VISUAL: Animation — one key duplicating across a city grid of doors, each clicking open.]**

The NSA kept that key for over five years. They never told Microsoft the lock was broken.

And then, in 2016, the unthinkable: a mysterious group calling themselves **The Shadow Brokers** breached the NSA's own arsenal — and dumped its cyberweapons onto the open internet. For free. For anyone.

**[VISUAL: Pastebin-style leak post. Headlines: "NSA HACKING TOOLS LEAKED".]**

The master key to the world's locks was now lying in the street.

Somebody picked it up.

**[MUSIC: pulse quickens.]**

On the morning of May 12th, 2017, someone welded EternalBlue to a piece of ransomware and released it. The result was called **WannaCry** — and it did something no ransomware had ever done before.

It didn't need you to click a link. It didn't need you to open an email. It *hunted*. Every infected computer automatically scanned the internet for other vulnerable machines, broke in through EternalBlue, encrypted everything… and kept spreading. A virus in the truest sense of the word.

**[VISUAL: Infection-spread animation — one red dot becoming thousands, time-lapse counter of infected machines.]**

One machine became a hundred. A hundred became ten thousand. Within *hours*: Britain's National Health Service — crippled. Telefónica in Spain — down. FedEx, Renault, the Russian Interior Ministry, train stations, universities, police departments.

Two hundred thousand machines. A hundred and fifty countries. In one day.

Security teams at the biggest companies on Earth were watching their dashboards turn red with no idea how to stop it.

And Marcus Hutchins? He was on vacation. Sleeping in.

**[BEAT — record scratch of silence.]**

He woke up at 2:30 in the afternoon, checked the UK's cyber-threat feeds, and realized the internet was on fire. So he did what he always did with a new virus.

He caught a copy of it… and took it apart.

## ACT 3 — TEN DOLLARS AND SIXTY-NINE CENTS [10:00 – 14:30]

**[VISUAL: Re-enactment — dark bedroom, three monitors, code scrolling. Energy drink cans. Clock: 15:03.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Inside WannaCry's code, Marcus found something strange.

Before encrypting a single file, the malware tried to contact a website — a long, gibberish address, something like *iuqerfsodp… .com*. Forty-two random characters. A domain that didn't exist.

Every infected machine on Earth was knocking on the door of a website that had never been registered.

Marcus didn't know *why*. But he knew one thing, because it was his standard playbook for tracking botnets: **if malware talks to a domain nobody owns — you buy the domain.** That way, every infected machine reports to *you*, and you can map the outbreak in real time.

**[VISUAL: Namecheap-style checkout screen. Cursor hovers over "Complete Order — $10.69".]**

So at 3:03 p.m., between bites of lunch, Marcus registered the domain.

Cost: **ten dollars and sixty-nine cents.**

**[SFX: mouse click. Silence.]**

Within seconds, his new website was receiving thousands of connections per second from all over the planet. He was watching the biggest cyberattack in history in real time.

But then… something else happened. Something Marcus didn't understand yet.

**[VISUAL: Infection counter animation — the exponential curve suddenly bends… and flatlines.]**

The infections *stopped*.

Not slowed. **Stopped.**

Here's the twist buried in WannaCry's code: the malware checked that gibberish domain and asked one question — *does it exist?* If the answer was no, it attacked. If the answer was yes… it shut itself down. Every copy, everywhere on Earth, permanently.

The malware's author had built in an off-switch — most likely as a trick to detect security researchers — and then bet the whole operation on nobody ever finding it.

Marcus had found it. By accident. For the price of a large pizza.

**[BEAT.]**

At first, he panicked — for a horrifying hour, he thought registering the domain had *triggered* the encryptions rather than stopped them. Imagine that: sitting in your childhood bedroom wondering if you just nuked the NHS.

Then the test results came back. And the truth landed:

The attack was dead. And he was the one who killed it.

**[VISUAL: Headlines cascading: "ACCIDENTAL HERO HALTS GLOBAL CYBERATTACK". News anchors b-roll, BBC/CNN chyrons.]**

Within twenty-four hours, journalists tracked him down. Camera crews camped outside his parents' house in tiny Ilfracombe. The anonymous blogger **MalwareTech** became *Marcus Hutchins, the man who saved the internet* — on every front page on the planet.

The British government publicly thanked him. Companies offered him jobs. Strangers offered to buy him drinks for life.

He was twenty-two years old. And he had just made one very dangerous mistake.

**[STING — cut to black.]**

He'd lost his anonymity.

And somewhere in a federal building in Milwaukee, Wisconsin… an FBI agent read the news, looked back down at an open case file, and underlined a name.

## ACT 4 — LAS VEGAS [14:30 – 18:30]

**[VISUAL: Las Vegas strip at night. DEF CON badge b-roll, crowds, neon.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Three months later. August 2017.

Marcus flies to Las Vegas for DEF CON — the world's biggest hacker conference. And this year, he's not just an attendee. He's royalty. The hero of WannaCry. A week of parties, penthouse suites, shooting ranges, and people lining up for selfies.

On August 2nd, his vacation ends. He's at McCarran Airport, sitting in first class on the runway, phone in hand… when he's pulled off the plane.

**[VISUAL: Re-enactment — airport hallway, three figures approaching. Audio drops to a low hum.]**

Three men in plain clothes. One question: *"Are you Marcus Hutchins?"*

Handcuffs.

The man who saved the internet was now in FBI custody — and the charges had nothing to do with WannaCry.

**[VISUAL: The actual indictment document — "United States of America v. Marcus Hutchins". Highlight the word "KRONOS".]**

The indictment said that years earlier, Marcus had created a banking trojan called **Kronos** — malware built to steal online banking credentials — and that an associate had sold it on the dark web's most infamous marketplace.

And here's the uncomfortable part of this story. The part that doesn't fit the hero headline.

**It was true.**

**[BEAT. Let it sit.]**

Remember the teenager on those forums, writing password stealers to impress older hackers? That road hadn't ended there. In his late teens, Marcus had written code — including pieces of Kronos — for a partner who sold it to actual criminals. He was paid in bitcoin. He knew what it was for. He'd spent years trying to leave that life behind, terrified it would resurface.

He became the world's best malware killer… partly because he used to be one of its builders. The same skills. The same locked doors. Just standing on the other side of them.

**[VISUAL: Split-screen — MalwareTech blog on one side, dark-web market listing on the other.]**

The internet, which had crowned him a saint in May, now had to decide what he was. Some called him a fraud who'd fooled the world. Others pointed out the obvious: the FBI had just arrested the one person who stopped an attack on *hospitals* — for something he did as a teenager, and had clearly walked away from.

Even his prosecutors seemed conflicted. Security legends publicly raised money for his defense. Meanwhile Marcus was stuck in the US for *two years*, banned from leaving, facing up to ten charges and decades of theoretical prison time.

In 2019, exhausted, he pleaded guilty to two counts. And admitted everything.

## ACT 5 — THE SENTENCE [18:30 – 21:00]

**[VISUAL: Milwaukee federal courthouse, exterior. Quiet, no music.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
July 26th, 2019. Sentencing day, Milwaukee.

Federal sentencing is where stories like this usually end in a number. Five years. Eight years. The government itself acknowledged the case was complicated — but the guidelines gave the judge room to bury him.

Judge J.P. Stadtmueller was seventy-seven years old, one of the longest-serving federal judges in America. And what he said that day almost never happens in a courtroom.

He looked at the case — the teenage crimes on one side, and on the other, a man who had, in his words, turned the corner *before* he was ever caught… and then saved untold thousands of systems, hospitals among them.

**[VISUAL: Court transcript excerpt on screen, key line highlighted as it's read.]**

*"It's going to take the people like [you]… to eliminate this entire subject of the woefully inadequate state of internet security."*

Translation: the world needs you outside more than it needs you inside.

**Sentence: time served.** One year of supervised release. No prison.

**[BEAT.]**

Marcus walked out of that courthouse a free man — a convicted felon, and a hero, at the same time. Because that was the truth all along. Both things.

**[VISUAL: Present-day — MalwareTech YouTube/Twitter b-roll.]**

Today, Marcus Hutchins is still doing the only thing he's ever really done: taking malware apart and showing the world how it works — now for millions of followers. The kid from the hacking forums became one of the most trusted voices in security.

And WannaCry? The U.S. government later formally blamed it on state-sponsored hackers from North Korea. The kill-switch domain Marcus bought for $10.69 is still registered, still running — because if it ever goes dark, dormant copies of WannaCry around the world could wake back up.

Somewhere, right now, a ten-dollar website is still quietly holding the leash on the biggest cyberattack in history.
